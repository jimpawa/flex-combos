Three comprehension questions, per prototype

Questions 1 and 2 are identical in all four cells — they are the baseline you compare across prototypes. Question 3 is the one that changes, because it is the only one that tests the on-screen sentence. Every quoted string and price below is taken verbatim from the live builds, card fx-2 (FC Salzburg v Pafos FC).

The asymmetry that matters: in the loss-framed builds the number in the sentence is the complement of the number on the pill — “2 picks can lose” sits directly beside a control reading Flex 1/3. Two different numbers, same card. In the ratio-framed builds the two numbers match (Flex 2/3 → “2 or more”). Q3 exists to measure whether that mismatch costs comprehension.

Three corrections to the draft wording

1 · “legs” becomes “picks”

These builds run with __PICKS = true, so every string in the product says picks — “2 picks can lose”, “Combo · 3 picks”. Writing 3-leg bet 3-pick combo keeps the question in the participant’s vocabulary instead of teaching them a second one mid-test.

2 · “Normal Combo” becomes “No Flex”

There is no “Normal Combo” anywhere in the UI. The control reads Normal Combo No Flex, and the rails are labelled “Popular Combos” / “Flex Combos”. Asking about a label that does not exist tests recall of your internal naming, not comprehension of the design.

3 · Q1 is a concept check, not a reading check

In the No Flex state the card shows no explainer sentence — the line only appears once a flex level is chosen. So Q1 cannot be answered by reading the screen; it measures the mental model they leave with. Worth logging separately from Q2 and Q3.

Q1 · Same in all four prototypes

1Concept · No Flex vs Flex

What is the main difference between placing a combo with No Flex and placing the same combo with Flex turned on?

  1. AWith No Flex every pick has to win. With Flex you can still win even if some picks lose.Correct
  2. BWith No Flex you can change your picks after placing the bet. With Flex they are locked in.
  3. CNo Flex combines your picks into one bet. Flex splits them into separate single bets.
  4. DNot sure — I don’t know the difference.

Why C is the distractor to watch: it is the “system bet / splitting into singles” mental model carried over from other operators. If C scores highly, the concept is landing as a stake-splitting mechanic rather than a tolerance mechanic. D is a real signal, not a throwaway — report it rather than folding it into “incorrect”.

Q2 · Same in all four prototypes

2Payout · the cost of flexing

Shown with the card in view: No Flex 4.68 · Flex 2/3 2.34 · Flex 1/3 1.35

You switch this combo from No Flex to Flex 2/3. All three of your picks then win. What happens to your payout compared with leaving it on No Flex?

  1. AIt is lower — I get paid at the Flex price even though every pick won.Correct
  2. BIt is higher, because Flex is an extra feature I added.Key trap
  3. CIt stays exactly the same whether or not I flex.
  4. DNot sure how flexing changes the payout.

This is the commercially dangerous one. A participant picking B believes flexing is a free upgrade and will feel cheated at settlement. Keep the three real prices on screen — the question is whether they can read the trade-off with the numbers in front of them, which is the softest possible version of the test. Failing it here is a hard fail.

Q3 · Version A — for the “2 picks can lose” builds

Use in one-rail (and its alias one-rail-lose) and two-rails (alias two-rails-lose).

3Wording · loss framing

A 3-pick combo shows Flex 1/3 on the control, and underneath: “2 picks can lose and you still win.”

How many of the 3 picks have to win for this bet to pay out?

  1. A1 of the 3Correct
  2. B2 of the 3Key trap
  3. CAll 3
  4. DNot sure — the two numbers on the card don’t seem to agree.

B is the whole reason this question exists. The card shows a 2 in the sentence and a 1 on the pill, so a participant who reads the sentence’s number as “must win” lands on B. Every B here is a direct cost of loss framing, and it is the number to hold against Version B below. Option D is worded to give people permission to report the conflict rather than guess — treat a high D as the same finding as a high B.

Q3 · Version B — for the “1 of 3 must win” builds

Use in one-rail-ratio and two-rails-ratio.

3Wording · ratio framing

A 3-pick combo shows Flex 1/3 on the control, and underneath: “1 or more picks have to win”

How many of the 3 picks have to win for this bet to pay out?

  1. A1 of the 3Correct
  2. B2 of the 3Compare vs A
  3. CAll 3
  4. DNot sure — the two numbers on the card don’t seem to agree.

Deliberately the same stem, options and correct answer as Version A — only the quoted sentence changes. That is what makes the two cells subtractable: any gap in the A-score is attributable to wording alone. Expect this version to score higher, because the sentence’s 1 matches the pill’s 1. If it does not score higher, the problem is the x/y pill itself, not the sentence — a much bigger finding.

One caveat on the layout variable

Three questions can’t separate one rail from two

Why the table above repeats Q3 within each wording pair

  • Rail layout does not change a single word on the card — one-rail and two-rails show identical sentences and identical prices.
  • So any comprehension difference between them would be noise, not signal.
  • What the layout actually changes is whether people find the flexable stock at all: one rail mixes 5 cards, two rails surfaces a dedicated “Flex Combos” rail of 10.
  • Measure that behaviourally — time to first Flex interaction, scroll depth before it, share of sessions that never touch the control.
